# Teardown framework

The full diagnostic. Work through it in order — each layer's answers change how you read the next one. Not every section applies to every post; skip what does not apply and say you skipped it rather than padding.

The questions here are chosen because they produce non-obvious answers. If a question yields something you could have written without watching the video, you have not answered it yet.

## 1. Hook autopsy (0–3s)

Most of the outcome is decided here, so give this more attention than feels proportionate to three seconds.

Capture, verbatim and precisely:

- **The first spoken line**, exact words, original language.
- **The first frame** — describe what is actually on screen at 0:00, not the general vibe. Who or what, at what scale, in what setting.
- **The on-screen text**, exact, plus where it sits and how it enters.
- **The first cut** — when does the frame change? Under 1s reads as urgency; over 3s reads as confidence, and both work, for different reasons.

Then the diagnostic questions:

**What tension does it open, and when does it close?** Almost every functioning hook opens a gap and withholds the resolution. Name the specific gap ("mostra o resultado e esconde o método") and find the timestamp where it closes. If it closes at 0:04, the video needed a second hook — find it. If it never closes, the payoff was the comment section or the profile visit, which is a different strategy worth naming.

**Does it start mid-motion or from rest?** Starting inside an action already underway is one of the most reliably transferable techniques there is, because it removes the setup the viewer would have scrolled through. Note which one this is.

**What is the claim, and is it disputable?** Hooks that stake a position people might argue with generate comments. Hooks that promise a payoff generate watch time. Look at which one this is and check whether the engagement ratios agree — a disputable claim with almost no comments means the claim was not actually contested, and the reach came from somewhere else.

**Could you scroll past it and lose nothing?** Be honest. Many hooks in high-performing posts are mediocre, and the post worked for other reasons. Finding that is more useful than manufacturing praise.

**Is the hook doing work the algorithm rewards, or work the human rewards?** A hook engineered purely for a 2-second retention spike (jump cut, loud noise, flashing text) can win distribution while leaving the viewer with nothing. Note the difference — the second kind builds an audience, the first kind builds a view count.

## 2. Beat map

Build a timestamped table of the whole piece. This is the single most useful artifact in the teardown and the thing that most distinguishes a real analysis from an impression.

For each beat: start time, duration, what happens, what is on screen, and what it is doing structurally (hook, re-hook, proof, turn, payoff, CTA, loop).

What to look for once it is built:

**Where are the re-hooks?** Long-form retention is a series of small re-openings. Find the moments that restate the stakes or introduce a new question, and note their spacing. Frequent, regular spacing suggests a deliberate structure worth copying.

**Where is the payoff, and how much video remains after it?** A payoff at 80% with a long tail usually bleeds retention. A payoff at the very end, or a loop that sends the viewer back to 0:00, is a different design. Say which one this is.

**Does it loop?** Check whether the last frame connects to the first. Deliberate looping inflates watch time substantially and is fully replicable, which makes it worth flagging every time you see it.

**What is the rhythm curve?** Not average cut rate — the *shape*. Dense-then-slow, slow-then-dense, or steady. The moment the rhythm breaks is almost always the moment the content turns; check whether they coincide. When they do, that pairing is the most copyable thing in the piece.

**Where does the density of information peak?** Compare it to where the rhythm peaks. When the fastest cutting sits on top of the densest information, viewers rewatch — which the algorithm reads as a strong signal and the viewer experiences as a good video.

## 3. Visual grammar

Describe the system, not the aesthetic. "Clean and modern" is not analysis.

- **Format and framing** — aspect ratio, where the subject sits, how much of the frame is empty, whether the composition leaves room for platform UI (captions, buttons, the bottom third).
- **Typography** — the actual treatment: weight, size relative to frame height, placement, entrance animation, and whether text is redundant with the audio or adds a second channel. Text that says something different from the voiceover is a distinct technique.
- **Color** — the palette in a sentence, and what makes it legible on a small screen at low brightness. Note contrast, because most social video is watched in bad conditions.
- **Camera** — static, handheld, or moving; and whether movement is motivated or decorative.
- **Cut style** — hard cuts, match cuts, whip pans, speed ramps. Note the *most repeated* transition rather than cataloguing every one.
- **Production floor** — what is the minimum equipment and skill that produces this? This directly determines whether it lands in "replicável" or "condicional" later, so answer it concretely: phone and a window? A tripod and an afternoon of editing? Motion design skills?

## 4. Sound

Consistently underweighted in content analysis and consistently decisive.

- **Trending sound or original audio?** If trending, when did it peak — riding a sound early is a distributional advantage that will not be available to the user later, which makes it conditional at best.
- **Does the piece survive on mute?** A large share of feed viewing is silent. If the video is incoherent without sound, that is a real constraint on its reach and worth stating.
- **Voiceover treatment** — pacing, energy, whether it was written to be spoken or read off a script. Scripted-sounding VO reads as an ad; conversational VO reads as a person. Note which.
- **Sound design** — whooshes, hits, risers on transitions. Cheap to add, disproportionately effective at holding attention, almost always replicable. Flag it whenever present.
- **Silence** — a deliberate gap before a payoff is a strong technique and rare enough to be worth calling out.

## 5. Caption and comment engineering

**The caption's first line** is the only part most people see. Quote it exactly and ask what job it does: repeat the hook, extend it, add the context the video omitted, or ask a question. Each is a different strategy.

**Is the caption load-bearing?** Sometimes the video is the hook and the caption is the content. That is a distinct format, particularly on Instagram and LinkedIn, and it changes what the user should build.

**Structure** — length, line breaks, emoji use, where the CTA sits. Line breaks are deliberate; a wall of text and an airy structure perform differently.

**Hashtags** — count and specificity. Mostly a weak signal now, but a sharp change from the account's usual pattern is worth noting.

**The pinned comment** — frequently where the real CTA lives, or a deliberate seed for discussion. Always check.

**The comment section itself**, which is the richest and most-skipped source in a teardown:
- What are people actually saying? Quote 3–5 verbatim. Their language is the audience's language and it is directly usable in the user's own copy.
- Are they arguing, asking, tagging friends, or praising? Tagging drives reach hardest; arguing drives comment volume; asking reveals a content gap the user could fill.
- Does the creator reply? Reply activity lifts distribution and is entirely replicable.
- Any attribution tells — "vim pelo @fulano", references to a repost or a news story.

## 6. Positioning — does the reach serve the brand?

Ask it plainly: **a person who watched this and nothing else — what do they now believe about this brand?**

Sometimes the answer is "nothing," and the post was hollow reach. That is a legitimate and important finding, especially when the user is about to copy the format. Reach that does not accrue to positioning is a treadmill.

Also check: does the content match what the brand sells? Is there a path from watching to caring? Does the post's top comment suggest people understood what the brand does, or just enjoyed the video?

## 7. Carousels and static posts

The framework above is video-shaped. For carousels, substitute:

- **Slide 1 is the hook.** Same autopsy — what tension, what claim, is it scrollable-past.
- **Slide 2 is the retention test.** The largest drop in any carousel is 1→2. What makes someone swipe? Usually an explicit incompleteness on slide 1.
- **The beat map becomes a slide map** — one line per slide: what it says, what it shows, what it is doing. Look for whether each slide ends by opening the next.
- **The last slide** — CTA, loop, or summary. Check whether it earns the swipe it took to get there.
- **Design system** — type scale, grid, color, negative space, and whether it is consistent enough to be recognizable in a feed without the handle.
- **Save rate is the metric that matters.** Carousels that get saved are reference material; carousels that get liked are entertainment. Different products.

## 8. Analyzing a flop

Underperformance is often more instructive than a hit, and users bring these too. Same framework, different question: not "what worked" but "where did it lose them."

The usual suspects, roughly in order of frequency:

- **The hook took too long to start.** Setup before payoff. Check the first 2 seconds against the account's better posts and you will often find the whole answer there.
- **The tension never opened.** The video announced its topic instead of opening a gap.
- **It was legible only with sound**, and most of the feed was silent.
- **The payoff did not match the promise.** Check the comments for the tell — disappointment reads clearly.
- **Wrong audience reached.** Compare the comments to the usual commenters; if they are strangers who did not get it, distribution was fine and targeting was not.
- **Nothing was wrong with it.** Variance is real and large. If a post is within normal range for the account and the craft is sound, say that rather than manufacturing a diagnosis. Telling someone their fine post was fine is a service.
